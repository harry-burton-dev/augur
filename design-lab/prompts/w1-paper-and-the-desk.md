# Wildcard W1: Paper and the desk. Work you can pick up and hand over.

Read `design-lab/brief.md` first; it holds the rules, kit, critique format
and run shape. When you start, move this file to
`design-lab/runs/<date>-paper-and-the-desk/prompt.md`.

---

The cinematic run and batch 1 all look at screens through other screens
(film, instruments, maps). This wildcard looks at the opposite: the
**paper tools** people still trust for high-stakes work, and the physical
gestures that come with them. Air traffic controllers ran the world's
busiest airspace on paper strips for decades; hospital wards run on
whiteboards; planners run factories on T-card boards; accountants built
double entry on ledgers. These tools are fast, shared, glanceable and
honest about ownership in ways most software isn't. Find out what AUGUR
would gain by behaving like a well-run desk. Work in
`design-lab/runs/<date>-paper-and-the-desk/`.

You may argue against an existing concept (for example that depth and blur
are the wrong metaphor for work that is handed between people) if the
research supports it. Say so in the critique.

## Direction
- **Ownership is physical:** at any moment one person holds a thing, and
  everyone can see who. Handover is a deliberate gesture.
- **Position is meaning:** where an item sits (which bay, which column,
  how far it sticks out) says its state without a badge.
- **Marks are additive:** you annotate, initial, stamp and strike through;
  you never silently overwrite.
- Explain, never hide; many deployments; calm at rest (from the brief).

## References and what to take from each
- **Air traffic control flight progress strips** (UK NATS and others,
  before and alongside electronic strips): one strip per flight, held in
  bays by sector, ordered by time; a controller "cocks" a strip (sets it
  askew) as a reminder; strips are physically passed to the next sector;
  annotations by hand in a fixed shorthand. Research the move to
  electronic strips (for example NATS EFPS) and what controllers insisted
  on keeping.
- **Hospital ward whiteboards and handover sheets** (SBAR handover:
  situation, background, assessment, recommendation). Take: a standard
  shape for handing something to someone else.
- **T-card planning boards and kanban's origin at Toyota**: cards in slots,
  colour by type, a board as the single shared truth. Take: capacity you
  can see as empty slots.
- **Double-entry ledgers**: every change recorded twice, balanced, never
  erased; corrections as new entries. Take: an audit trail people read.
- **Library card catalogues and Zettelkasten** (Niklas Luhmann): one idea
  per card, links by number, the catalogue as an index into the shelves.
- **Rubber stamps, initials and seals**: approval as a visible mark with a
  name and date on the object.
- **Abigail Sellen and Richard Harper, *The Myth of the Paperless
  Office***: why paper survives (spatial layout, annotation, handover).

## Surfaces (6 variants each)
1. **Desk language sheet** (grammar): strip, bay, cock, hand over, stamp,
   initial, strike, slot, board; token recipes and what each must never mean.
2. **Home "what needs you"** as a strip bay: what you hold, in time order,
   what you've cocked, what is coming to you.
3. **Approvals** (publish a document to a department): the stamp, who
   initialled before you, what you're signing for.
4. **Handover** between people and floors: pass an intent or a disputed
   fact to someone with an SBAR-style note.
5. **Team board** (a Team floor's work): T-card board with visible
   capacity and owners.
6. **Corrections and audit**: a ledger view of a fact or document where
   every change is an entry and nothing is erased.

Round 2: combine into 3-4 screens. One must show a handover between two
people end to end, from both sides.

## Questions the shortlist must answer
- Which paper gestures map to a mouse and keyboard without feeling like
  skeuomorphism for its own sake?
- Where does this agree or clash with focal planes and the light language?
- What AUGUR would need to record (holder, handover, initials) that it
  doesn't today.
