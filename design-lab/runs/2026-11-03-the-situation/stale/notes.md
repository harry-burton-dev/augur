# Surface 6 · Stale context

**The moment:** WS-31 is opened on 24 Nov, three weeks after Tom assembled it, by Leah Morgan, who has taken it over. In those three weeks:
- **10 Nov:** N7 went back to its old route, so two pieces now hold for N4 only.
- **14 Nov:** Tom moved to Housing. The driver story was said only to him, so its one witness has moved post.
- **18 Nov:** Brightwell withdrew the offer of 20 Oct. The new offer is two years at 4% a year more.
- **20 Nov:** Brightwell revised its October count to 23, accepting the 4 calls with photos.
- **21 Nov:** November passed 20, so clause 7.2's service failure has happened. The projection came true.

Of 14 pieces, 8 moved and 6 still hold, and one reading has been added (23 missed so far in November). Each change carries one of the reference desk's **five reasons an answer moves** (something happened, we learned about the past, who can answer changed, a guess was settled, a word changed meaning).

**Keys:** R replays, T shows the admin words. Each page lists its own keys.

---

## V1: Since 3 Nov
*Idea.* The set reopened, every piece with a state chip: **no longer holds**, **its one source has moved on**, **changed**, **new since** or **still holds**, each with its date and its reason. Changed pieces show the old text struck through, the new text, and why. The piece that no longer holds offers its replacement ("Use 'Renewal offer, 18 Nov'") or set aside. The orphan offers to ask the outside party or the person who moved. What still holds is listed last, quiet.

*Sources.* The reference desk's five reasons an answer moves; wear and time's visible repair; the catalogue's stale hatch.

*What works.* It answers the prompt's "what has changed and what no longer holds" in one scan, sorted by what needs a person. The five reasons make "changed" honest: the revised count is *we learned about the past*, not *something happened*, and a reader can tell those apart. "Its one source has moved on" is a state no other run had: the claim hasn't changed, but whoever could vouch for it is gone.

*What fails or risks.* Eight changed cards with struck text is a lot to read on a Monday. The summary bar's five counts compete with each other. It doesn't show *when* things changed relative to each other (V3 does).

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm: chips appear once. Dark when normal: the pieces that hold go quiet. No letterbox.

*Needs.* The conditions watched by the events service; the five reasons on every change; an "orphan" state when a piece's only source leaves.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V2: Holds while
*Idea.* Each piece's condition is a breaker on a panel ("holds while the offer of 20 Oct stands"). When its condition breaks, the breaker trips with the date and the event that tripped it. Only a person resets it, by reading the piece again. The log on the right lists the five events and how many pieces each tripped. Two conditions AUGUR can't watch yet are named.

*Sources.* Electrical breaker panels (each circuit labelled, a trip is visible and stays tripped); conditions precedent in contracts.

*What works.* "A breaker trips on its condition, not its age: the July count is weeks old and still holds" is the key idea of the surface. **Staleness is a broken condition, not elapsed time.** That makes "holds while" the most important field a piece has. A tripped breaker stays tripped until a person looks, which is the right default for context. Naming what AUGUR can't watch carries over the briefing's *watches say what they can't see*.

*What fails or risks.* A panel of 14 switches is a physical metaphor that may read as settings you can flip. It needs every condition written so a machine can watch it, and most won't be.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm: trips land once; a tripped panel is still, not blinking. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Conditions as watchable predicates where possible (a document withdrawn, a count changed, a person moved, a date passed). The events service clock (from the briefing run).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 4

## V3: Three weeks, one scale
*Idea.* The three weeks on one time scale. Each piece is a line from when it was added. Each event is a vertical cut, labelled at the top. A changed piece's line turns blue (repaired in place) after its cut; a piece that no longer holds turns dashed. A now line sweeps from 3 Nov to today.

*Sources.* Marey's train graph; wear and time's one time scale and now line.

*What works.* It shows clusters: four pieces moved in the last week, all from Brightwell's side. And it shows what moved before the handover (the route) against after it. The sweep is a good moment and plays once.

*What fails or risks.* Most of the chart is plain grey lines, so the signal is a few marks on a wide page. Labels for events a day apart collide (they are staggered). The piece names truncate at 1280.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: good. Calm: one sweep. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Event times on every change (already in the change record).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V4: The re-check
*Idea.* Reopening runs a challenge-and-response check over **only what moved**. Each item shows "the set said" and "it is now", with the reason. The person answers *keep the new reading*, *replace* or *set aside*, and the next item comes up. Pieces that hold are listed and not re-read. When all are answered, the set becomes version 2 and a decision can rest on it.

*Sources.* Aviation checklists (challenge and response); the apprentice's "nobody re-checks for you".

*What works.* It turns "the set is stale" into a short task with an end: 8 answers, not 14 re-reads. Every answer is recorded with who gave it, so the decision can say what version it rests on. It is the action half of V1.

*What fails or risks.* Defaults ("Keep the new reading") invite a tired person to press Enter eight times. The defaults should be per state, and *replace* should need its replacement opened first. Checklists feel bureaucratic for small sets.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: good. Calm: answers land one at a time. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Versioning on the working set; a recorded answer per changed piece.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V5: The errata slip
*Idea.* The set as issued, never edited, with an errata slip laid in. Each line reads "*for* … *read* …", with its date and reason, and a dagger marks each changed line on the page. A new reading is an *add* line.

*Sources.* Printers' errata slips and corrigenda; the edition (S) prompt's errata.

*What works.* It keeps what the first reader read exactly as they read it. That matters when a decision was taken on version 1, and it is the best answer to "what did Grace actually see?". "For … read …" is old, short and unambiguous.

*What fails or risks.* A slip over a page is two places to read; past ten errata it becomes the main text. The rotated slip is a gimmick (slight, but still). It is better as the version history than as the working view.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Immutable versions; a diff rendered as "for … read …".

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 3

## V6: What left with Tom
*Idea.* Staff rotate. An overlap strip shows Tom holding the set from 3 to 17 Nov and Leah from 14 Nov, with three days when both hold it. Three columns follow. **Kept**: every reason in Tom's words. **Said, not checked**: claims whose only witness has moved post. **Only in his head**: what happened that no record holds, here a phone call on 7 Nov with the supplier about a recovery plan. It is drawn as a gap with a name and an *Ask Tom for a note* button.

*Sources.* Military relief in place (the outgoing and incoming unit overlap; continuity books); scheduled rotation as a known outage.

*What works.* It makes the background principle concrete: **anything only in someone's head is a scheduled outage**, and the handover is the last cheap moment to ask for it. The overlap strip puts the readback run's *the readback moves it* on a time scale. The "only in his head" column is something no other variant thought to draw.

*What fails or risks.* AUGUR can't know what's in Tom's head. The gap exists only because the handover asked "did anything happen that isn't in the set?" and Tom answered. That question is the real feature, and the column is how its answer shows.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm. No letterbox.

*Needs.* A handover step that asks the outgoing owner for unrecorded events; owner history on the working set.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## Ranked
1. **V2 Holds while**: staleness is a broken condition, not age.
2. **V1 Since 3 Nov**: what changed, with the reason.
3. **V4 The re-check**: only what moved, then a new version.
4. **V6 What left with Tom**: the handover's last question.
5. **V5 The errata slip**: what the first reader saw, kept.
6. **V3 Three weeks, one scale**: good as history, not as the view.

**What I'd build first.** "Holds while" as a required field on every piece. The events service watches the conditions it can (a document withdrawn, a count changed, a person moved, a date passed) and names the ones it can't. A tripped piece shows V1's chip and reason in place. Reopening runs V4 over only what moved, which makes a new version (V5 keeps the old one). The handover always asks V6's question.
