# Surface 5 · Two frames

**The moment:** Tom Reyes (contracts officer) and Mira Haddad (Team lead) read the same data two ways. **Frame A, "Brightwell is failing the contract"**: two months over the limit, complaints more than doubled, and the supplier says it is three drivers short. **Frame B, "Our route change made two rounds unworkable"**: 11 of the 27 misses were on N4 and N7, the two rounds redesigned on 2 Sep. Each frame predicts something different: A that misses spread over all nine rounds, B that they gather on the two changed ones. What settles it is the rise since July split by round: 8 of the 14 extra misses are on the changed rounds and 6 on the other seven. **Both hold in part.** A second, smaller disagreement sits underneath: Brightwell counts 19, not 27, because it leaves out 8 "no access" calls.

**Keys:** R replays, T shows the admin words. Each page lists its own keys.

---

## V1: Side by side, and what would settle it
*Idea.* Frame A and frame B either side of the data both look at. Each shows its reading, the cues it picks, and what we should see if it is right. In the middle, each shared piece carries a small bar per frame that uses it. Under them, the test that would settle it, run once. The split bar fills, the verdict appears, and both frames get "holds in part".

*Sources.* Klein, Moon and Hoffman's data/frame theory of sensemaking (2006); Weick, *Sensemaking in Organizations* (1995).

*What works.* It follows the prompt exactly: **both frames shown, not averaged, and what would settle it.** Showing each frame's prediction beside its reading is what makes the test possible: a frame that predicts nothing can't be tested. The middle column shows that the frames choose different cues from one shared set.

*What fails or risks.* "Run the test" suggests every disagreement has a query that settles it, and many don't. The verdict is written per test, which takes judgement. Two frames is the easy case.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm: one result. No letterbox.

*Needs.* A frame object: name, author, cues (pieces), prediction, and status (open, holds, holds in part, doesn't hold).

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V2: Same chart, two lenses
*Idea.* One chart with two lenses. Frame A lights the limit line, the months over it and the complaints. Frame B lights the route change and the bars split by round. Each lens dims what the other picks. Beside it, an **average is struck out**: "Missed collections rose for a mix of reasons", true and useless, because it hides that the frames predict different things.

*Sources.* Weick (frames select cues); map overlays.

*What works.* Lighting and dimming shows that a frame is *what you look at*, better than any text can. The struck-out average is the run's position on the prompt's "rather than averaging them", stated as a rule.

*What fails or risks.* It needs a chart where both frames' cues can be drawn. "Both" shows everything and gets busy. Dimmed marks at 12% are nearly invisible, which hides what the other frame sees, against explain, never hide. They should be dimmed, not gone (they are).

*Principles.* Focus stacking: good. Explain, never hide: fair. Calm: good.

*Needs.* Cue marks per frame as data, drawn by the chart kit part.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 5

## V3: From 27 to 19
*Idea.* The smaller disagreement (two counts of the same month) walked from one to the other like a bank reconciliation. Our count is 27. Less 8 "no access" calls gives Brightwell's 19. Of the 8, 4 have crew photos (agreed on evidence) and 4 are open. Beside it: the counts don't disagree about what happened, only about what to count. What would settle the last 4 is named and asked for. The two counts are never averaged: "23 would be a count of nothing."

*Sources.* Bank reconciliation; a statement of differences.

*What works.* For count disagreements it is the right form, and the most common one AUGUR will meet (two systems, two definitions). Every line is a reason with a number. It ends with what would close the gap and who has been asked. "Agreed on evidence" is a good middle state between disputed and settled.

*What fails or risks.* It only works for numbers that reconcile line by line; frames about causes don't. It is a specialist's form, though readable here.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Definitions on Data Atlas counts (what each source includes); the carried "sources disagree" mark.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V4: Data that won't fit
*Idea.* A table with what was seen down the side and the two frames across the top, each saying what it expects. Every observation is marked *fits*, *doesn't fit* or *doesn't say* against both frames. Each frame has exactly one row it can't explain. That is where it gets questioned: elaborate, question, reframe. A reframe that explains both misfits is offered in pencil, for both authors to sign or not.

*Sources.* Klein, Moon and Hoffman's sensemaking moves (elaborate the frame, question it, reframe); analysis of competing hypotheses (Heuer), used lightly.

*What works.* It shows *why* people change their minds, rather than just announcing that they should: a misfit is a row with the other frame's name on it. "Neither may drop a row because it doesn't fit" is the rule. "Doesn't say" stops a frame claiming evidence that is neutral.

*What fails or risks.* It is close to ACH, which the analyst's bench run (AA) owns. Marking fit is a judgement, and the author of a frame will mark kindly. The reframe in pencil is tempting for a model, and must stay pencil.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Per-frame fit marks per piece, by whom.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 3

## V5: What we agree
*Idea.* A statement of agreed facts and issues. The left side lists the facts both have initialled (6 pieces, two sets of initials each). The right side lists only what is still in dispute: three issues, each with both positions in their authors' words and **what would decide it**. The decider reads the agreed facts as fact and the issues as open.

*Sources.* Statements of agreed facts and issues in civil procedure; the Scott schedule (a table of disputed items with each party's position).

*What works.* It shrinks the argument to what is actually in dispute, and it is the most useful view to hand a decider. "Agreeing a fact is not agreeing what it means" keeps the rungs apart: people agree on data and information, and disagree on context. Initials are a small, familiar act.

*What fails or risks.* It needs both people to sit down and initial, and the run assumes they will. Initials look like approvals; the minute run's *agreed in the room* already separates assent to the record from agreement with the decision, and this should use the same marks.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Per-person assent on a piece (not an approval); the issue list as part of the working set.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V6: Agree the test first
*Idea.* Before the test is run, each person writes the result that would change their mind. Tom: "If more than half the rise is on N4 and N7, I'll accept the route change is a main cause." Mira: "If the other seven rounds rose by more than 4, I'll accept the supplier is failing too." Both are sealed on 30 Oct. The test runs once, by template, and **each rule applies itself**: both are met, and each frame is rewritten, with the old reading kept and struck through.

*Sources.* Double crux (find the result that would change both minds); pre-registration of hypotheses in research.

*What works.* "The rule applied itself; nobody had to win the argument" is the most humane line in the run. Writing the rule before the result removes the incentive to read the result your way. A frame is versioned like any other piece.

*What fails or risks.* People rarely know in advance what would change their minds; asking can feel like a trap. It works for testable frames only. Seals and pre-commitment can feel legalistic in a Team.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm: one reveal. No letterbox.

*Needs.* A "would change my mind" field on a frame, locked when the test is set; the test as a saved Explorer query.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## Ranked
1. **V1 Side by side, and what would settle it**: the base form.
2. **V6 Agree the test first**: how a frame changes without a winner.
3. **V5 What we agree**: what the decider reads.
4. **V3 From 27 to 19**: the form for count disagreements.
5. **V2 Same chart, two lenses**: the best picture of a frame.
6. **V4 Data that won't fit**: leave it to the analyst's bench (AA).

**What I'd build first.** A **frame** as a kind of working-set piece: an author, a reading (context, signed), the cues it rests on, a prediction, and a status. Two frames sit side by side (V1), each with a "would change my mind" line written before any test (V6). The decider's view is V5: agreed facts above, issues below, each with what would decide it. Count disagreements use V3's reconciliation. Never show an average of two frames.
