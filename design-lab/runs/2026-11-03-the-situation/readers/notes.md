# Surface 4 · Same information, three readers

**The moment:** one piece of information, *missed collections doubled since July and over the contract's limit for two months running*, becomes different context for **Mira Haddad** (Team lead, Collections North: "What can my crews change this week?"), **Grace Obi** (Head of Environment and Streets: "Do we renew, and on what terms?") and **Owen Pryce** (COO, chairs the Contracts board: "Is any contract putting the service or the budget at risk?"). Each reader has a Mandate intent on their own floor (M-114, M-208, M-031). What each can open behind it follows the floor rule from docs/atlas.md: a floor reads what is kept on it and on the floors above. So Owen, on the Org floor, can open 1 of 2 records behind his paragraph, and the rest say whom to ask.

**Keys:** R replays, T shows the admin words. 1, 2 and 3 change the reader where a page has them.

---

## V1: Three columns, one band
*Idea.* The shared information once, as a band across the top with its chart. Under it, each reader has a column with their question, their paragraph (the nut graf), what they can do and by when, the intent it serves, the pieces it is drawn from, and what they can open behind it, with a strip of solid and hatched cells.

*Sources.* The military common operational picture (one picture, filtered per role); the nut graf.

*What works.* The band says the facts are shared. The columns say the meaning isn't. "Owen can open 1 of 2 records behind it; the rest: ask Tom Reyes" carries the briefing run's *reach before act* into context. Each column's "drawn from" list shows that each reader gets a different slice of the same set.

*What fails or risks.* Nobody sees all three columns in the product except the person assembling them. Like the briefing's "three readers", this is a publisher's view. The columns are mostly empty below the fold at 1600.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong (hatched reach, whom to ask). Calm: columns settle once. No letterbox.

*Needs.* A reader list on the working set and a context paragraph per reader.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V2: One picture, three overlays
*Idea.* A shared base picture (missed collections by month) that never changes, with each reader's acetate laid over it. Mira's adds the route change, the two rounds' bars and the revert. Grace's adds the limit line, the offer's validity and the decision date. Owen's adds a strip of 14 contracts with one over, and the board date. 1, 2 and 3 lay them on and off.

*Sources.* The common operational picture; map overlays on acetate (each staff cell draws on its own sheet over one map).

*What works.* "An acetate adds marks; it can't move the base" is the right rule for per-reader context: nobody's reading changes the facts. Together, the three acetates are the situation, which is where the common operational picture got its name. It looks good and stays quiet.

*What fails or risks.* Three overlays at once clutter the chart (the 20 Nov and 1 Dec labels crowd). It needs a chart-shaped base, and much context isn't one. Floor colours as overlay colours compete with the line.

*Principles.* Focus stacking: good (an acetate is a plane). Explain, never hide: good. Calm: yes.

*Needs.* Overlay marks as data per reader (events, limits, dates), drawn by the kit.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 5

## V3: One template, three readers
*Idea.* One paragraph template. The fixed part is the information (the same words and digits for everyone). The slots are filled per reader (lead, meaning, so-what) and light up when they change. A table beside it checks that every shared digit appears the same in all three copies.

*Sources.* Tilden ("relate to the visitor"); audience-specific labels; the briefing run's same digits at every height.

*What works.* It is the honest mechanism behind V1: per-reader context is **slots on a shared template**, not three free-written paragraphs. The digit check makes it safe to let the slots differ. It shows exactly what changes for whom, at a glance, and that is the review a publisher needs.

*What fails or risks.* Slot writing is still per reader. The check can only cover digits in the shared part; a slot's own number ("1 of 14 contracts") isn't checked against anything. The underline styling is busy.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Calm: slots light once per change. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Paragraph templates with named slots; the same-digits check as a kit part (from the briefing run).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V4: Your floor and the one above
*Idea.* A reader's context is the information placed against the intents of their own floor and the floor above. Mira reads it against M-114 (Team) and M-208 (Department). Grace reads it against M-208 and M-031 (Org). Owen reads it against M-031 alone, and the page says so: "No floor above the Org". Each intent gets a line saying how the information bears on it.

*Sources.* Mission command, "intent two levels up" (US Army ADP 6-0); Bungay, *The Art of Action*; the batch's *intent travels two levels* concept.

*What works.* It gives "relative to someone" a rule AUGUR can compute: who the reader is (floor) decides which intents frame the information. That makes per-reader context *derivable* rather than hand-written. It is the bridge to the intent run (AC).

*What fails or risks.* It assumes every floor has one relevant intent; real floors have dozens. The "bears on" lines are written per intent, which is as much work as per reader. It doesn't handle people whose question isn't an intent (a curious reader).

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: good. Calm: yes. No letterbox.

*Needs.* Intent relevance for a working set (which intents it serves, per floor).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 4

## V5: What it changes for you
*Idea.* For each reader, two columns: what they would do *without* the information and what they would do *with* it. Where it changes something, it is context for them. Where it doesn't (Owen: "The same."), it is information for that reader, and the page says so instead of raising its voice: no notice, no badge.

*Sources.* Bateson's difference that makes a difference; value of information in decision analysis (Howard, 1966).

*What works.* It gives "is this context for you?" the sharpest test in the run, and it is the rule that keeps per-reader context *quiet*: if it changes nothing you'd do, you don't get told. That sits squarely with dark when normal and the room-tone run's *answer, not announce*. "It lights again only if something it rests on moves their decision" makes a fine watch rule.

*What fails or risks.* "What would you do without it?" is a counterfactual someone must write, and people will skip it. It flatters the assembler's view of each reader.

*Principles.* Calm: strongest in the surface. Explain, never hide: good (the Org's row stays, quiet). No letterbox.

*Needs.* A "changes what I do?" flag per reader per working set, set by that reader or the assembler.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 5

## V6: Floor travel
*Idea.* The information sits sharp and still in the middle. Floor travel (↑ ↓, or the rail) racks each floor's context into focus in that floor's colour, with the floor above or below soft behind it.

*Sources.* The cinematic run's floor travel and focal planes; rack focus.

*What works.* It uses movement people already know from AUGUR (floor travel) for a new purpose, so it costs no new idea. The information never moves, which makes "it's the same information" physical.

*What fails or risks.* The blurred plane is unreadable (the same objection as rungs V6). Only two floors fit at once. It shows one reader per floor; a floor with several readers breaks it.

*Principles.* Focus stacking: strong. Explain, never hide: weak. Calm: good.

*Needs.* `--x-proposed-far-blur`.

*Scores.* Clarity 3 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 5

## Ranked
1. **V3 One template, three readers**: the mechanism.
2. **V5 What it changes for you**: the test, and the quiet.
3. **V1 Three columns, one band**: the publisher's view.
4. **V4 Your floor and the one above**: makes "relative to someone" computable.
5. **V2 Three acetates**: good when the base is a chart.
6. **V6 Floor travel**: right gesture, wrong reading.

**What I'd build first.** V3's template: the information as fixed text with checked digits, and per-reader slots, each signed. V4's rule picks the default readers: the floors whose intents the set serves, plus the floor above. Then V5's test decides who is told: a reader whose actions it doesn't change gets nothing, and the set files it as information for them.
