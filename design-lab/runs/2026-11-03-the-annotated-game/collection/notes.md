# Surface 5 · The collection

**The moment:** 9 November 2027. The book holds twelve agreed reviews across two floors, filed by situation. Two belong to bodies that keep them to those who took part. Leah Grant, a contracts officer who joined in September, is looking for games like the one she is about to play.

**Shared data:** `DATA.book`: families and codes (the deployment's own, C1 to C5, S1 and S2, P1, with C3 split into C3.1 to C3.3), the facets that define a position (what it changes, depends on, one-way step, trial), motifs with their tone, and twelve games with result, days, facets, motifs and reach. Codes, names and facets are data; nothing in the pages names them.

**Keys:** ↑ ↓ code or motif (V1, V4), ← → chapter or step (V3, V5), selects (V2), click a spine (V6), R replays.

---

## V1: The book
*Idea.* An opening encyclopaedia for decisions. A tree of families and codes with counts; each code a page: its defining facts, its book line ("1. Paper with cover counted · 2. Trial with an end date · 3. Lines anyone can read · 4. Hire, then 5. give notice"), the deviation that went wrong (`4. Notice, then 5. hire ?`, R-07), its games with results, and its theory (answers to questions reviews asked). The moment opens C3.2.

*Sources.* ECO codes (Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings, 1974 onwards); Informator's opening index; said in a sentence (catalogue run).

*What works.* "Filed under the situation it was, not the subject it was about" is the key decision of the surface. A code is something a newcomer can recognise before playing it. The book line is the most compressed teaching the run produces: one line a new starter can follow. Locked games are listed with why and whom to ask.

*What fails or risks.* Codes need an owner per deployment and will drift (is a decision C3.2 or C5?). A book line from three games is thin theory and should say so. A tree of codes is a taxonomy, and people don't browse taxonomies; they search (V2).

*Principles.* Many deployments: the codes are data. Explain, never hide: locked games shown. Calm.

*Needs.* A situation catalogue per deployment (a Mandate setting, owned by someone); reviews filed under a code; theory entries.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V2: Find by position
*Idea.* Search by where you stand, in one sentence of selects: "a change to [a contract] that depends on [hiring], where the step you can't undo is [a notice period], made [as a trial]". Results are cards with a match bar (how many facts match) and the facts themselves. A game with a different code that matches every fact is marked as a transposition: R-04, the out-of-hours phone line moved to a contractor (C5), "our own night staff were given notice before the contractor had hired its night team".

*Sources.* Transpositions in opening theory (different move orders reaching the same position); said in a sentence; de Groot (experts recognise positions, not subjects).

*What works.* The transposition is the run's best argument for positions over subjects: a phone line and van inspections are the same game. Facts that match are lit; facts that don't are muted, so the reader sees why a game is here. Locked games are counted, not hidden.

*What fails or risks.* Four facets is a small vocabulary; real positions differ in ways facets miss (size, who the contractor is). The sentence with selects is charming at four facets and awkward at eight.

*Principles.* Said in a sentence. Explain, never hide. Calm.

*Needs.* Facets recorded on decisions when they are drafted (learning V1 uses them to recognise the position).

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V3: Our best games
*Idea.* An anthology edited by a person: four chapters (order of moves, trials not bets, lines that fire, cover), two games each, a turning point drawn and one line on what each teaches. Opens on the editor's foreword. "Edited, not generated. A chapter is added when two reviewed games teach the same thing."

*Sources.* Kasparov, *My Great Predecessors*; Nimzowitsch, *My System*; Chernev, *Logical Chess: Move by Move*.

*What works.* People read books someone chose; nobody reads a database. Twenty minutes for the floor's lessons is the right size for induction. Chapters are motifs with an editor's voice.

*What fails or risks.* Needs an editor with time, which most floors don't have. The mini curves for games other than R-07 are drawn as samples here. An anthology goes stale; it needs a date and an owner.

*Principles.* Calm. Explain, never hide: who chose and why.

*Needs.* A curated list per floor (Artifacts collection that cites reviews by `Ref`); an editor role.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V4: Motifs
*Idea.* The patterns that recur across reviewed games, each with its opposite where one is named ("one-way step first" against "what it depends on, first"), the games that show each, how they ended, and a base: dots for each game, "3 games: 0 kept, 1 amended, 2 reversed" against "3: 2 kept, 1 amended, 0 reversed", and "Too few to call a rate. Read the games; don't count them. A motif is a reviewer's name for a pattern, not a cause."

*Sources.* De Groot's chunks (masters recognise patterns); tactics trainers sorted by motif; the answer has a base (reference desk run).

*What works.* Pairs of motifs teach better than single ones: the contrast is the lesson. The base line keeps it honest. Motifs travel across codes (one-way step first appears in C3.2, C3.3 and C5), which is how a floor learns something general.

*What fails or risks.* Motif names are a vocabulary someone must own, or every reviewer invents new ones. With three games per motif, the dots will be read as a rate however hard the text says not to.

*Principles.* The answer has a base. Explain, never hide. Calm.

*Needs.* A motif list per deployment with an owner; motifs on reviews.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## V5: The explorer
*Idea.* An opening explorer for C3 (bring work in-house). Step by step through the situation (a costing is on the table, the meeting decides, something must be in place first, a line is crossed), and at each step what was played next in the book, how many games, a results bar (kept, amended, reversed) and which games. At step 3: "Secure it, then the one-way step ! · 3 games" against "The one-way step, then secure it ? · 2 games". Always: "5 games is not a rate."

*Sources.* Opening explorers in analysis tools (moves played from a position with result bars); the answer has a base.

*What works.* It is the most direct way to show a newcomer "what people did here and what happened". Every bar shows its count, and each count opens its games.

*What fails or risks.* The steps are a generic script for C3; each code needs its own, and hand-writing them is costly. A results bar invites causal reading ("secure it first → kept"), which five games can't support. Of the six, this is the one most likely to mislead.

*Principles.* The answer has a base: shown, maybe not enough. Explain, never hide.

*Needs.* Steps per code (part of the book line); moves in reviews tagged with which step they are.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 3

## V6: The shelf
*Idea.* The whole book as shelves by floor (Department floors, Org floor), spines sized by critical moments with a result dot. Reviews you can't open stay on the shelf as hatched spines with their unit and code; selecting one says why ("its body has not agreed the review yet; drafts stay with those who took part") and whom to ask. A third shelf, "Yours", holds Leah's own reading notes, which nobody else sees and which are never filed.

*Sources.* Library stacks; explain, never hide; mine versus ours.

*What works.* It answers "what stays private" visually: locked reviews exist and say so; personal notes have their own shelf. "No shelf by person. The book can't be searched by who played a move." A pleasant way to see a floor's history at a glance.

*What fails or risks.* Spines are a skeuomorph and won't scale past fifty reviews; the shelf is an overview rung, not a working view. Vertical text is hard to read.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strongest of the six. Mine versus ours. Calm.

*Needs.* Reach on reviews (follows the decision), personal notes in Artifacts (private).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## Ranking
1. **V2 Find by position**: positions over subjects, with transpositions.
2. **V1 The book**: codes, the book line and theory.
3. **V4 Motifs**: patterns in pairs, with their base.
4. **V3 Our best games**: the induction read.
5. **V6 The shelf**: the reach rules, best kept as rules.
6. **V5 The explorer**: the most likely to be misread.

## What I'd build first
The book as a Mandate view: find by position (V2) as the way in, landing on the code's page (V1) with its book line, games and theory, and motifs in pairs with their base (V4) on the same page. V6's reach rules apply throughout. V3 is a curated Artifacts collection, built when an editor exists.
