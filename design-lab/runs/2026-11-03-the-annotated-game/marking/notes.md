# Surface 3 · Marking

**The moment:** 24 November 2026. Owen Pryce, an improvement lead from the Org floor who played no move in D-118, runs the review with six people who did. Each marks the critical moments of the finished decision; the meeting agrees the review on 8 December.

**Shared data:** as in the grammar notes. `DATA.review` holds the after-action review (why, keep, change, each pinned to a move). Marks are drafts (pencil) until released, and ink once the meeting agrees.

**Keys:** ↑ ↓ choose a move, 1 to 6 mark, 0 clear, Enter release (V1); Enter releases yours (V2); P pins (V3); Space moves the room on (V4); click a quiet move to try a line, Esc to leave it (V5); G turns the model gateway off (V6); R replays.

---

## V1: Six keys and a line
*Idea.* Keyboard first. Choose a move, press 1 to 6 for the mark, write one sentence (140 characters). Signs (`□ ⊕ N ∞`) are buttons under the keys. The line has a hint that changes with the mark ("A ? needs what else was on the record. Without it, ?! at most.") and three prompts from the record: known then, chosen, instead. Everything is pencil until Release; the footer says exactly who can see it.

*Sources.* Informator symbols; keyboard annotation in analysis boards; pencil and ink.

*What works.* Marking is fast, which matters: a review that takes an afternoon won't be held. The hint ties the mark to the rule from the grammar (a `?` needs an alternative on the record), so the discipline is built into the input, not a training course. World moves can't be marked, and the panel says why.

*What fails or risks.* The 140-character line will be ignored by people who want to write a paragraph; the counter shows it but doesn't stop it. Number keys for marks are learnable but not discoverable; the labels under each key help. Sign buttons don't toggle in the prototype.

*Principles.* Pencil and ink. Explain, never hide: who sees the draft. Calm.

*Needs.* A `MarkPicker` part (Quill); review drafts with per-person visibility.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V2: Your own moves first
*Idea.* Jo Barnes's view. Her moves in D-118 are listed (only one: "Assumed: two fitters by 30 June"). She marks it first, in pencil: `?!` "I said it to keep the meeting moving. I hadn't checked the market." The reviewer's mark is a sealed, hatched card: "Owen Pryce has marked this move. You'll see it when you release yours." She releases; Owen's `?` appears beside hers, and a note: one step apart, both stay; "the difference between 'I didn't check' and 'nobody checked'".

*Sources.* Kasparov and Botvinnik annotating their own games; the after-action review's rule that those who were there speak first; blind second marking in exams.

*What works.* The strongest privacy and fairness idea in the run. The mover isn't judged before speaking; the reviewer isn't anchored by the mover. Showing both, signed, is more honest than a single verdict, and the gap between them is often the lesson. "Reply in one line" lets her answer without changing the mark.

*What fails or risks.* Sealing the reviewer's mark is a small "hide", justified (it's an anti-anchoring rule, it says what is sealed and when it opens) but it must stay visible as a sealed card, never absent. People who have left can't mark their own moves (Priya, later), and the review should say so.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: the sealed card says who and when. Mine versus ours: the mover's draft is hers. Calm.

*Needs.* Per-move ownership (who made the move), sealed marks, release and withdraw, replies.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 5

## V3: The after-action review
*Idea.* The four after-action questions as four columns: what was meant to happen (the decision's own words and lines), what happened (from the records: fitters 1 of 2, overdue 7 of 18, £148, 175 days, reversed), why, and what to keep and change. Every why and every keep or change is pinned to a move, and the pin becomes the move's mark. A strip of move chips shows which moves are pinned. The moment pins the second "why" to move 6.

*Sources.* The US Army after-action review (four questions, no blame, run as routine); Kasparov's "what to keep" in his game notes.

*What works.* It makes marking a by-product of the routine people already know. "A cause that can't be pinned to a move isn't one yet" is a good rule: it stops vague whys ("communication"). The first two columns are filled by AUGUR, so the meeting starts at "why".

*What fails or risks.* Four columns at equal width give the record columns the same weight as the reasoning; "why" deserves more room. Pinning a keep to a move works; pinning a change to a move is sometimes forced (the cover lesson is about a costing, not a move).

*Principles.* Explain, never hide. Calm. The workings show (the "what happened" column is records).

*Needs.* A review record type with why, keep and change items linked to moves.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V4: Agreed in the room
*Idea.* The review is agreed at the next meeting the way the minute run agrees a minute. Each critical moment is read back with its marks; each person present answers "that is the review" (faces light as they do). Move 13 is agreed with two marks: `!?` from Owen and `?` from Ade. The rules beside it: silence isn't assent; a disagreement is recorded, not settled; the mover's own mark always stays; once agreed marks are ink, corrections are new dated marks; agreeing files it in the book.

*Sources.* Agreed in the room (minute run); Hansard's agreed record; dual-annotated games, where two masters' notes sit side by side.

*What works.* "Assent is to the record, not to the mark" is the right line: Marcus can agree the review contains a `?` on his move without agreeing with it. No vote on marks, ever. It reuses a ritual the floor already has.

*What fails or risks.* A meeting's agenda time is precious; reading back four moments takes ten minutes, which is right for a reversal and too much for a routine decision. Six faces per item is noisy; a count would do once everyone has answered.

*Principles.* Agreed in the room, extended. Calm. Explain, never hide.

*Needs.* The minute run's assent mechanism, reused for reviews.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 5

## V5: Four lines for seventeen moves
*Idea.* Commentary is rationed to the moments found. A meter shows six moments (two found by the records, four by people) and four lines used. Clicking a quiet move (9, "Bay fitted out") opens a side panel: "Bay fitted out wasn't found critical", and three choices: make it critical (and say what else was on the record), mark it without a line, or leave it. The records' two moments carry a sentence by template, not a person's line.

*Sources.* Nimzowitsch and the Informator practice of commenting only at critical moments; the reader's minute (briefing run).

*What works.* The budget isn't a number someone sets; it is the moments that were found. That keeps reviews short without a limit anyone can game. The note on the bay ("Most reviewers want to praise the bay. The mark says that.") is the kind of editorial rule a review needs.

*What fails or risks.* A rule against commenting feels patronising to people who want to say thank you; the mark `!` has to be enough, and a "thanks" reply could sit outside the review. A meter of slots invites filling them.

*Principles.* Calm: less text. Explain, never hide: why a line isn't allowed here.

*Needs.* Critical-moment finders (grammar V5) as a service; nothing else.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V6: Found by rule, marked by people
*Idea.* The records' rules put six candidates on the table, each with its reason chips (one-way step, falls two steps, line crossed, no answer for 38 days, date moved and line not) and its position. The model drafts one sentence for each in pencil, from the records only. The reviewer keeps a candidate, marks it, or bins it. The side panel says what each voice did, including what the records couldn't find (move 4: an untested assumption leaves no trace until it fails). G removes the drafts and nothing else.

*Sources.* "Learn from your mistakes" modes in analysis tools, which find the moments and leave the lesson to the player; pencil and ink; the apprentice run's model as one step of several.

*What works.* The division of labour is right: rules find, people judge, the model drafts at most. "Turning the model gateway off changes the work, never the review" is testable. The candidates give a reviewer a starting point on a long decision.

*What fails or risks.* The model's drafts here add little over the reason chips; the honest finding is that the model may not be needed for reviews at all. Candidate cards for world moves ("A world move: no mark") are noise; group them with the move that answered them.

*Principles.* Pencil and ink. Explain, never hide. Calm.

*Needs.* The finders as a service over a decision's events; the model gateway's pencil slot (optional).

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 4

## Ranking
1. **V2 Your own moves first**: the fairest rule in the run.
2. **V1 Six keys and a line**: the input, fast enough to be used.
3. **V3 The after-action review**: the routine that makes marking happen.
4. **V4 Agreed in the room**: how a review becomes ink.
5. **V6 Found by rule, marked by people**: candidates without the model.
6. **V5 Four lines for seventeen moves**: an editorial rule, best as a default.

## What I'd build first
A review mode on a Mandate decision that opens as an after-action review (V3), with the marking panel (V1) on each move, and the mover's own mark first and the reviewer's sealed until then (V2). Candidates found by rule (V6, without the model) seed the critical moments. The meeting agrees it like a minute (V4). V5's rule ships as a default, not a meter.
