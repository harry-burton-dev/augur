# Surface 1 · The intent sheet (grammar)

**The moment:** Hollin Water, a regional water company, after a dry summer. The Org intent (ORG-07, Priya Raman, Chief Executive) is to get through the winter without household restrictions. Network Operations (DEP-14, Dana Whitlock) rests the Thornley reservoirs by moving demand onto boreholes. The North Zone team (TM-31, Sam Okafor) feeds the zone at night through the Kelda link, and Ellis Marsh, a network controller, holds the part that keeps that feed full. At 06:10 on Tue 3 Nov, Ashby borehole 3 goes out of supply on a quality alert and tonight's plan is 8 Ml/d short.

**Data:** `DATA.intents` (four nested intents with purpose, done looks like, key tasks, limits with owners, freedom, branches and assumptions, plus six siblings, one restricted), `DATA.parts` (the eight words, with the doctrine words one step down), `DATA.klein`, `DATA.states`, `DATA.plan.options`. Keys are shown in each top bar; **R** replays and **T** shows the doctrine words under the plain ones on every page.

**Words the run settled on** (plain first, doctrine with T): *Purpose* (purpose), *Done looks like* (end state), *Key tasks* (key tasks), *Limits* (constraints and restraints), *Freedom* (disciplined initiative), *Serves* (nesting), *Said back* (backbrief), *Drift* (cross-track error, revisit line). Nothing in a person's view says "commander", "mission" or "enemy"; the civilian words carry the same ideas.

## V1: The order sheet
*Idea.* The intent as a five-paragraph order: 1 Situation, 2 Intent (purpose, done looks like, key tasks), 3 Limits, 4 Freedom (with the branch plan), 5 Who (holder, deputy, what it serves, who has said it back, drift, review). Situation comes first because what's going on frames what we want; the method is absent on purpose and the page says so.

*Sources.* The five-paragraph order (situation, mission, execution, support, command and signal); ADP 6-0's commander's intent; Klein's intent statement.

*What works.* It is the whole record in one sheet, and it answers the prompt's first question directly: these are the fields Mandate needs. Inherited limits are drawn with a double bar and their owner, so "a floor may tighten, never loosen" is visible without a sentence. The situation paragraph is built from the items the intent touches, which ties the record to the morning rather than leaving it a filed slogan.

*What fails or risks.* Five paragraphs is a lot for a Team intent with two tasks. The situation paragraph goes stale by lunchtime; it must say when it was built. Order language ("paragraph 3") is a military smell for a civilian reader, which is why the numbers are quiet and the words plain.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: every limit names its owner. Calm: deposits once. Focus: one paragraph lit at a time (1–5).

*Needs.* An intent record with these fields and an `inherits` list; a fact binding on each end-state line; `--x-proposed-ceiling` (limit bar, carried), `--x-proposed-freedom`.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V2: The marks
*Idea.* A specimen sheet of the nine marks intent leaves at text size: the intent chip, the said-back ring (hollow, half, full), the limit bar (single own, double inherited), the freedom edge, the "so that" thread, the within-intent stamp, the four verdicts, the drift stroke and the hatch for an intent you can't open. Space builds one line from them, as the Department reads Ellis's change at 07:52.

*Sources.* Type specimen sheets and map legends; the apprentice run's maker's mark and lamp; the label run's hatch.

*What works.* The closing line is the run's argument in one sentence: who changed what, within which intent, so that what, how many limits held, and what it cost. Every mark is a shape before it is a colour, so it survives colour blindness and the floor colours (the chip's bar is the only floor colour used). The ring borrows the room-tone run's question and answer: an intent passed down is open until said back.

*What fails or risks.* Nine marks is near the limit people can learn; drift and "against a task" could be confused (both are warm, dashed). The within stamp gets long when it names the freedom; it truncates.

*Principles.* Calm: the marks are still; the only motion is the sentence assembling once. Explain, never hide: the hatch names the holder.

*Needs.* `--x-proposed-thread`, `--x-proposed-freedom`, `--x-proposed-drift` (new); pencil, unseen, ceiling, standby, lit (carried). An `IntentChip` and `Ring` in Common.

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 5

## V3: Seven pieces, five fields
*Idea.* Klein's seven pieces of a good intent statement (purpose, objective, sequence, rationale, key decisions, anti-goals, constraints) drawn into the five fields Mandate keeps. For each field, a good line from DEP-14, a line from Dana's first draft that looks like it but isn't, why, and the one test the field must pass ("if the plan breaks, could someone choose a new one from this line alone?").

*Sources.* Gary Klein, *Sources of Power* (1998) on intent statements; ADP 6-0.

*What works.* It justifies the form: seven boxes become five because rationale is purpose and anti-goals and constraints are one list of limits with owners. The "looks like it, isn't" card is the most teachable thing in the run; "Run Ashby flat out" as a purpose fails the test visibly. The tests become the help text in the writing surface.

*What fails or risks.* A reference page, not a working screen. Dropping "sequence" loses something when order genuinely matters (it survives as a key task's order). People may still put method in key tasks.

*Principles.* Plain words first; doctrine with T.

*Needs.* Nothing new; the tests per field as kit copy.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 3 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 4

## V4: The life of an intent
*Idea.* Six states, each marked as a question or an answer: Draft (the owner asks), Passed down (a question to each holder), In force (answered), Worth revisiting (a question to the owner), Done, Lapsed. DEP-14's real history runs underneath: two catchball drafts, agreed under ORG-07, two teams say it back, South Zone still open. At 1.2 s the 06:10 alert crosses an assumption and the intent moves to "worth revisiting": Dana is asked once (keep or amend), the work goes on, and nothing is paused.

*Sources.* The room-tone run's question and answer; the minute's revisit lines; Hoshin Kanri catchball.

*What works.* "In force for the teams that said it back" is a sharp rule: an intent nobody has confirmed understanding of can't unlock freedom yet, so South Zone's changes still ask. "Asked once, not again" keeps a crossed assumption from becoming a nag. The state is per holder, which is what makes the backbrief matter.

*What fails or risks.* Per-holder state complicates the simple "is it in force?" question. "Lapsed" needs a decision about who renews (the review date asks). The rail reads as a process diagram; in the product it is a line on the intent page, not a page.

*Principles.* Question and answer: strong. Calm: the move is one step, asked once. Explain: the open holder is named with the date.

*Needs.* Intent state per holder; assumptions as watched records (the minute's revisit lines) with "knowingly crossed".

*Scores.* Clarity 4 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 4 · Fit 5

## V5: So that
*Idea.* The why-how ladder. Ellis's part at the bottom, the Team and Department intents above it in full, the Org folded to one line you can open. Each rung names the one key task above that it serves. Up answers "why?", down answers "how?". Beside it, the chain said in one breath: "I set up the Kelda feed each evening so that North zone carries itself at night so that Thornley rests this winter so that we get through the winter without restrictions."

*Sources.* ADP 6-0 nesting ("understand intent two levels up"); the mission statement's "in order to"; why-how laddering in value-focused thinking.

*What works.* "Said in one breath" is the test of a nest: if it doesn't read as reasons, the nesting is wrong. Naming the task served gives "so that" an address, which every later surface uses (ranking, the change record, the notice). Two up in full and further up folded is the prompt's "two levels up" made into a rule with no hiding.

*What fails or risks.* A strict chain assumes every part serves exactly one task above; real work often serves two. Folding the Org may read as "not your business" to someone who wants it; the fold must be one click.

*Principles.* Focus stacking: the selected rung is the near plane. Explain: the fold says it can be opened.

*Needs.* `serves` (intent and task id) on every intent; `--x-proposed-thread`.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 4 · Feasibility 5 · Fit 5

## V6: The filter
*Idea.* Intent as a filter on options. Tonight's six ways to fill the 8 Ml/d gap fall through three layers: limits stop (and name the owner), freedom sorts (outside it, ask the person who holds that choice), purpose orders (options that spare Thornley first; one that costs it says how much). They land in four bins: within, within but against a task, ask, across a limit.

*Sources.* Michael Bratman, *Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason* (1987): an intention filters which options are admissible; the BDI model.

*What works.* The four verdicts are the run's most reusable idea, and they are decided by rule from the record, never by a model. "Within, against a task" is the honest middle that every approval scheme lacks: allowed, and the cost said. Nothing is filtered out of sight: the limit bin is shown with its owner, which is Bratman's filter made explain-never-hide.

*What fails or risks.* Real options aren't neatly pre-listed; someone has to write them (a person, or a model in pencil from the branch plan). Checking "outside freedom" needs freedom written precisely enough to test; vague freedom ("use Ryecroft if you have to") can't be filtered.

*Principles.* Explain, never hide: strong. Consequence preview: each option says what it costs the intent.

*Needs.* Limits and freedom as structured, testable clauses (quantity, unit, range, owner), not only prose.

*Scores.* Clarity 5 · Distinctiveness 5 · Feasibility 3 · Fit 4

## Ranking
1. **V6 The filter**: the four verdicts, decided by rule, are what "act within intent" runs on.
2. **V1 The order sheet**: the record Mandate needs, in an order that puts the situation first.
3. **V5 So that**: two levels up as a rule, with an address for every "so that".
4. **V2 The marks**: the kit's vocabulary at text size.
5. **V4 The life of an intent**: per-holder state and the once-asked revisit.
6. **V3 Seven pieces, five fields**: the reasoning behind the form, and its tests.

**What I'd build first.** The intent record as V1 lays it out (purpose, done looks like with a fact on each line, key tasks, limits with owners and an `inherits` list, freedom as testable clauses, branches, assumptions, serves), the chip and ring from V2, and V6's four verdicts as a rule in Mandate. V3's tests become the help text when writing.
